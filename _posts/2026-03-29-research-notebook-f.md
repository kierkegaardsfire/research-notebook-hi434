## The Unavoidability of Distortion
While completing the tutorial in QGIS I reflected on the readings about the nature of spatial history, and considered the ways in which my work on Prince Edward Island may be feeding into the “cartographic generalization” which Monmonier describes in his article. At each step in the cartographic process the map creator makes decisions that alter our perception of the actual physical space being portrayed. This begins at the most fundamental level - in class we discussed Mercator map projections which reshape and resize the world in order to project it onto a 2 dimensional plane that is easier for us to view. As we create more and more complex maps, we continue to make decisions for the benefit of the viewer which may seem innocuous, but taken together can distort our perception of the real space we are seeing. When the tutorial utilized a map from 1927 as a raster layer, I noted how it dramatically changes the viewers reaction to the map from a sleek, modern representation to a dated and fun map that looks like it belongs  in a tourist guidebook.

Although the 8 categories of data underlying the map had not changed at all, I could sense that as a casual viewer of the maps, I would have a vastly different reaction to one over the other, without realizing or understanding why I had that reaction. I would implicitly be more inclined to trust the accuracy of data from a map that looks like it was designed in a GIS software than an antique, hand drawn map. On a similar note, in class we discussed how common mapping softwares such as Google and Apple maps that we use in our everyday life can be subject to political and cultural considerations that most users would be completely unaware of. Likewise the choices made by these corporations are legitimized because of their dominance in the field, as they rely on sophisticated satellite technology and software engineers to build and maintain their vast map software. Although there are open source alternatives, no student in our class could name them, indicating the strong level of dominance that large tech companies have in shaping how we view our world.
One of the Digital Humanities GIS projects which caught my eye before class was Yale University’s “HGIS de las Indias.” As a double major in History and Spanish, I was fascinated by the depiction of colonial latin america from a variety of lenses: *Iglesia*, *Minería*, and *Territorios*. I noted that the major categories of the map reflected imperial motives for colonization (God, Gold, and Glory), a bias that the website itself recognizes when it acknowledges that population records are incomplete due to lack of data for certain areas, and that mining data is collected from royal records and may more accurately reflect colonial administrative centers as opposed to mining towns.

Just as HGIS enabled ¨de las Indias¨ to canvas the continent with crosses to represent the Christianization of Latin America (a name which itself reflects the linguistic assimiliation of the continent), QGIS equips the user with a variety of tools to create historical narratives through maps.  In the tutorial, I utilized the “symbology” section of QGIS simply to remove an annoying red block from the design. But the function of these tools in practice is to “produce representational space” (White, pg. 5) through how we choose to represent the layers of a map.. Maps that seek to make an argument, such as connecting topography with property values in Rio, will undoubtedly use the aesthetics of their cartography to emphasize rather than diminish their point when presented with the opportunity. Because cartography requires simplification, the cartographer is sure to encounter many such opportunities throughout their work.

Nevertheless, these workshops and these readings opened my eyes to new perspectives about how we use maps both as historians and as everyday people trying to navigate our world. On a more fundamental level, they made me reconsider the nature of space and time in the human experience, as well as unexpectedly sparking an interest in the intersection between theoretical physics and the humanities. I am grateful to have had some of my most fundamental assumptions about the nature of human experience be challenged, and hope to carry these new outlooks with me so that I can take a more critical view of the representation of space throughout my life and academic work.
