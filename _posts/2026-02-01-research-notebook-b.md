<h4><u>Maximalism and Minimalism in Digital History</u></h4>
Revisiting the resources on minimal computing was likewise a reflective experience, as I realized the ways in which my own historical work, like that of Roopika Risam’s colleagues, had been “maximal.” In the developmental stage of my final research project, before I had made measurable progress of any kind, I planned to do as much as possible without regard for the time I had to do it or the skills and resources it would take. This realization made me reflect on how this pattern of thinking influenced other aspects of my academic work, such as an essay project which was, like my digital history exhibit, set in motion more than a year ago by an assigned reading on a piece of legal history.

It is clear that the method of conducting research can be self-destructive. But is minimal computing the answer? While going over the sources on the subject one thing that I kept considering was whether minimal computing was meant to be a recommended strategy for research, or simply a safeguard against resource scarcity - a kind of last resort. I think it is hard to argue with those who advocated for minimal computing as a means to expand resources for academic expression to all people. But to what extent should we allow ourselves to indulge in all of the vast resources available to us? As a American university student I am privileged to have access to an overwhelming amount of tools while conducting my research, and enjoy to utilize them in new, engaging, and yes, complex ways. Based on my own experience with these sources, I think that a large part of the tension Risam and Gil acknowledge has been caused by the subject of minimal computing is a reaction to the underlying feelings of guilt and, consequently, obligation, that emerge when those of us with privilege are confronted with the resource inequalities that work to our advantage.

Andreas Fickers’ article is a fun one to read. It is easy to get on board with the papers thesis as it takes us on a rollercoaster ride of elaborate initiatives aimed at promoting greater access to history. Between multilayered publication platforms, overlay journals, prismatic functionality and braided narratives - there is nothing “minimal” about Fickers and Darnton’s future of digital history. The idea of scalability is appealing because it doesn’t entertain a tradeoff: everyone can engage with history at their desired level - I can dive head first into the deep end with the comfort of knowing that others will have the option to go swimming too. As fun as it imagine, it is not hard to see how this kind of thinking can be problematic. By embracing scalability, we risk engraining and enabling existing divides in resource access, and narratives crafted by historic power imbalances. Although the two articles are by no means completely at odds, their does seem to be a dynamic comparable to the disagreement over, for example, the importance of equality vs. equity.
