# Research Notebook H
While working on my final project I was tasked with deciding on an appropriate CMS software to create my collection of civil rights documents. Having been recommended to Omeka and CollectiveAccess, I looked online for information about them and came across a lot of dense, hard to comprehend info packed onto their websites. The installation instructions for each software were contained not in one easy to access location but spread across multiple tabs and contained in websites and GitHub read mes that left me feeling a little overwhelmed. This is when I first turned to AI, simply to get advice on where to start. I described my project and asked which software would work best for me, to which Gemini responded that CollectiveAccess would suit my needs. Not knowing enough to disagree, I set about trying to install the software but again confronted a wall of information that I didn’t have the time or patience to try to wrap my head around.

At this point I scheduled a meeting with a tech consult at DH Hill library who has experience coding, and I figured that he would be able to give me an easy solution. Instead he was also a little lost so we went through the process of asking Gemini to simplify the steps for us together. We were working together for an hour with Gemini guiding us through the steps, often being confusing and taking us backwards in the process. We had to end the meeting due to schedule conflicts before we could finish, but I was confident that I would be able to quickly wrap up the installation process once I got home. In fact I would spend more than 4 hours at home working with Gemini to try to install the program. The most frustrating part of the experience which ended with me having a pretty bad migraine was that was I never really confident in my progress throughout the process. There was only one point, about three hours in, that I reached a sort of checkpoint or milestone that I knew I had made progress. Within the first three hours and the following hour I felt like I was playing blind darts, with no sense of whether Gemini had me on the verge of completion or was leading me down yet another dark alleyway that I would only have to spend more time to eventually get out of. One positive of the experience was that I did learn enough about the software I was working with that by the end I could sometimes tell when Gemini was making a mistake, and go look at the code in order to form my own conclusions and fix some problems without AI-assistance. 

Since installing the software and beginning the substantive work on my CMS project, I have continued to use Gemini. However, this use has continued to be for more mundane, trial-and-error coding fixes to enable me to accomplish my goals, rather than any kind of broad incorporation into my project. Unlike the intellectual merger of humanistic principles with AI demonstrated in the readings and example projects, my usage of AI really boils down to tedious problem-solving with unfamiliar software tools. Gemini acts a time-saving tool to help me get through the red tape so that I can focus on doing the substantive, humanities focused work of my project on my own.

That being said, my experience with AI did lead me to a better appreciation of Sternfeld’s discussion of AI as historian. I could relate to the concept of AI’s looking at the past to inform the future, but beyond that surface-level comparison I failed to grasp exactly what Sternfeld was getting at in any meaningful way. Throughout that reading I kept waiting for something that would deepen the analogy between the machine learning process and the historical method which to my mind never came.

One way in which I still interact with the content of the articles despite not using AI in a way which integrates it with the humanities is as a consumer of products like Gemini. The Data Worlds reading and our class discussion made me reconsider the nature of my role in this interaction. As a user of free AI software, Bode and Goodlad’s analysis did make me more weary of the ways I may be an unwitting “product” in the data driven political economy of AI tech, rather than the consumer I thought I was. The reading also opened my eyes not only to the concerns of being an unwitting source of data and learning for these tech companies but also how they access the vast amounts of data already necessary to run the programs before I ever open them. I was reminded of our discussions of Google Maps and related softwares from our spatial history unit in that my mind was opened to the power of data control in our modern world.
