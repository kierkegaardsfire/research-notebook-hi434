Since first completing this workshop I have had a significant amount of additional experience while revising my notebook and, in the process, creating animations, interactive displays, and a range of other creative choices as a part of this site. This experience has made me more familiar than I ever thought I would be with a website that in the past seemed like an impenetrable obsidian wall, reserved for people with coding knowledge. Subconsciously I thought it must be a very limited platform — how could this boring, simple website provide something of value to millions of users? Even now that I've gained significant experience with the platform, the many ways in which people around the world utilize the platform for their personal projects is still a mystery to me, and I hope that I can take my knowledge from this class and apply it to fields other than digital history, and beyond even the digital humanities as well.

GitHub is an extremely versatile platform that in one way or another reflects many of the themes brought up by the historians interviewed in "The Promise of Digital History." First and foremost, it is undoubtedly an amazing contribution to digital accessibility across a variety of fields, including history. This website is itself a testament to GitHub's contribution to open-source information. A few months ago I was struggling to create a simple repository on GitHub, and now I have a full-fledged website. With GitHub anyone — even the technically illiterate like myself — can now present their ideas with the world from their laptop, with huge freedom to express themselves through the open-ended nature of GitHub. Has this development contributed to "democratizing history"? If this class is any indicator, then it surely has. Not only has it enabled us as students to do what would have been the goals of a major institution a couple of decades ago, but we have also seen the proliferation of digital history with our own eyes through the countless projects we viewed as part of our units and while searching for inspiration.

The freedom offered by GitHub and open-source digital platforms like it has implications for how we as historians will conduct data. Seefeldt and Thomas' anecdote about the creator of [The Texas Slavery Project](https://www.texasslaveryproject.org/) quickly adapting the scope of their research to their newfound technological freedom is emblematic of what I and many other researchers have experienced when taking their first steps into the realm of digital history. With GitHub I went through a series of events where I continued to learn more about the platform, and then would hit a limitation on what I could do. I would resign myself to the fact that the site was indeed as limited as it seemed to me on first appearance, until eventually I would break through on that issue and move on to the next one. This learning process has been taking place for me throughout this semester and on a variety of other software platforms along with GitHub.

GitHub is also certainly representative of the apparent "impermanence" and "fluidity" of digital mediums. As an open-source platform, the content in GitHub's public folders can be accessed anywhere, by anyone, at any time, taken and repurposed. This is an incredible distribution of knowledge that, while fully possible, was not yet realized as these historians were interviewed in late 2008.[^1] The significance of this is a further reduction in "transaction and information costs" facing historians, and anyone else who has the wherewithal to utilize this open-source content. I reaped the benefits of this development when I used GitHub to install free, open-source software including CollectiveAccess, Omeka/Omeka S, XAMPP, Python, and a litany of others that I needed for varying tasks.

Although I feel lightyears ahead of the person I was in January when it comes to my digital capabilities, I'm sure that in these few months I've only scratched the surface of the potential of these tools. I hope to continue to expand my knowledge of digital history methods and of coding, software, and computers in general because I've seen how it can open up such interesting new avenues for expression and discovery.

[^1]: At that time GitHub was only a few months old, and was [in the process of launching GitHub Pages](https://github.blog/news-insights/the-library/github-pages/), and by the following summer would [reach 100,000 users](https://github.blog/news-insights/the-library/100-000-users/).
