While completing the “Simple Gephi Project” tutorial, I reflected on the readings about graph theory and network analysis, and the importance of network analysis in digital history. Whereas “The Network Turn” describes the origins of modern network analysis from military applicability, in the tutorial we see one example of the kind of humanistic application of network analysis which Ruth Anhert hoped for. 

Although it was an engaging and interesting introduction to network software, I found it difficult to see the potential significance in the Les Miserables network analysis. In the Les Miserables network, the nodes are the characters of the novel and the edges are simply describes by the tutorial as “who appears next to who” - in the same page or paragraph of Victor Hugo’s novel. The data is relatively simple, with the few categories including only two qualitative ones: name and gender, followed by a few measures of closeness that were not elaborated on in either the tutorial or the 1993 book which the data was originally published within. 

In both the readings and our seminar, one topic that puzzled me was the value of network analysis as a tool for historical research and analysis. On the one hand, the author of the “The Network Turn” maintains that “[t]he use of computational network analysis can lead to the creation of new knowledge,”  but goes on to reassume humanities scholars that the tool “supplements (but does not replace) traditional methods of inquiry” (Network Turn, pgs. 6-8). How does network analysis lead to the creation of new knowledge in the humanities and in digital history in particular? In our seminar I discussed the tension in the digital humanities between knowing enough about historical data to counter errors in computer interpretation, while knowing little enough that the use of computational tools can still provide valuable insight. 

	I think that network analysis can provide new insight and new knowledge to digital historians beyond its use as a merely demonstrative tool. I thought back to my own final project, which revolves around the historical correspondence between several interconnected historical figures.  I hope that by using network analysis to examine large data sets of communication and interaction between historic actors, we can bring a level of objectivity that will help diminish the influence of pre-existing narratives on our analysis.
