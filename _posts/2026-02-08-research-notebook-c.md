<h3 class="trinity-section-heading">Data: Processes and Power</h3>

<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p2">2</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">Just as procedural nuances in the law often lead to substantive implications, the significance of the mundane procedures of research is often unrecognized.<a href="#fn1"><sup>1</sup></a> Through this unit I gained a new appreciation for the power of data, which would be reinforced in later units. I had never seriously considered the notion of "raw data" before. I was susceptible to the impulse to treat an archive as an objective fact, and that mindset may have led to a lack of caution when treating my own data for research. There may have been ways in the past in which I have unknowingly "cooked" my data in significant ways that altered my research. The readings, in combination with Programming Historian's tutorial, imbued a newfound academic caution in me — if by nothing more than making me realize how little I understand about the nuances of information science in the first place.</span>

<h3 class="trinity-section-heading">Categories, The Problem Of</h3>

<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p3">3</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">Categories may be the most concrete example of how the practice of historical research changes the history we create.<a href="#fn2"><sup>2</sup></a> Erickson's article delves into the issue explicitly, advocating for a new kind of "flexible categorization" that utilizes technology to reexamine the preconceived bias of existing structures. OpenRefine serves to validate Erickson's claim that databases are tools to achieve this flexibility, as the tutorial demonstrates how GREL expressions can be used for "ad-hoc transformations" to target minute errors in large datasets.</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>
</div>


<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p4">4</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">Rosenberg implicitly shows the problem of categories as he pours over ECCO databases: sorting uses of "data" into "good" and "bad" before further sorting them into the categories which form the results of his research: "the term 'data' came into English in the early eighteenth century principally through discussions of mathematics and theology, roughly 70 percent of instances."<a href="#fn3"><sup>3</sup></a> What are "mathematics and theology," what corpus represents "English," and what "discussions" are we examining? As Erickson points out and Rosenberg would readily acknowledge, there are many layers of "uncertainty" underlying this claim that would go unevaluated if not for Rosenberg's self-criticism.</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>
</div>

<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p5">5</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">On my second runthrough of the Programming Historian's data-cleaning tutorial, one phrase which immediately caught my eye was the last of the tutorial's "four essential tasks" of data cleaning: <em>group together different representations of the same reality.</em> In the context of the tutorial, this refers to the "facetting and clustering" of metadata to uncover how different people over time chose to represent different ideas: one archivist's "transport-water" is another archivist's "transport - water."</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>

<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p6">6</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">Indeed, this is what Rosenberg engages in throughout his article by attempting to cluster the use of "data." Erickson encounters the limitations of clustering and faceting when she retraces her steps by keyword-searching her own categorization: the phrase <em>cognitive map</em> to represent a certain racialized use of the term "inner city." This reevaluation of her process resulted in Erickson uncovering a new perspective on the term's usage through the unique example of "William Higgins."</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>

<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p7">7</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">This quote had significance to me for a slightly different yet related reason. In my own research project, I had to create a workable cross-referencing system between two different NAACP archives — ProQuest and the Library of Congress. These databases contained the same data, but with completely different categories. Both were organized in a way that I can best describe as both chronological and subject-based, but the "subjects" and time periods were based on different value judgements made by different archivists. The result was a long and arduous undertaking for me, who did not have access to the original Library of Congress files.</span>

<span class="trinity-para-text">As you can see, my notes ranged from descriptive notes on my workflow to the occasional "54" — numbers or phrases which may have made perfect sense to me as I jotted them down, but were nonetheless only hours later nothing more than a cryptic digital breadcrumb trail. Reflecting upon this experience I realized that when I write notes like this, I subconsciously engage in a kind of "laziness calculus." I try, on the spot, to come up with the note that will take the least amount of effort while leaving a realistic probability that I can later on decipher its meaning and get the intended value out of the note. While more serious scholars may use more sophisticated methods than this, the end result is still that there will always be unintended consequences in the imperfect science of note-taking.</span>

<h3 class="trinity-section-heading">Note</h3>

<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p10">10</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">In a previous seminar, some of my peers brought up the idea of the "Violence of the Archive," a concept they had learned about from previous classes and with which I was unfamiliar. This unit's discussion of archival theory and the power of data and archives caused me to recall this passing mention. Finding a short article on the phrase in <em>English Language Notes</em>, I was led down a fascinating JSTOR rabbit hole on archival theory and the philosophy of language.</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>
</div>


<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p11">11</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">In 1995, Jacques Derrida wrote a reflection on archival power in which he analyzed the Greek root <em>arkhe</em>, which had two distinct meanings: <em>commencement</em> and <em>commandment</em> — either a starting point, or a source of power. The latter characteristic is demonstrated through the <em>archons</em> — Greek elites given the power to represent, declare, and make the law.<a href="#fn5"><sup>5</sup></a> It is interesting to note the similarity to Rosenberg's etymological analysis: data comes from <em>dare</em>, or "to give," fact from <em>facere</em>, "to do," and evidence from <em>videre</em>, "to see." Accordingly in English we use data to mean something given in an argument, fact to represent what has been done or has happened, and evidence for that which we can see to be true. As Rosenberg puts it: "facts are ontological, evidence is epistemological, data is rhetorical."</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>
</div>


<div class="trinity-para">
<span class="trinity-para-marker"><span class="pilcrow">¶</span> <a href="#p12">12</a></span>
<span class="trinity-para-text">Using Derrida's descriptions, then, it can be said that "facts" and "data" have a <em>nomological</em> principle, from Greek <em>nomos</em> (law) and <em>logos</em> (reason). In other words, they are perceived as being inherent, natural, or "before the fact," and thus wield significant authority.<a href="#fn6"><sup>6</sup></a> This category is thus related to Derrida's other, for which he and Rosenberg use the same term: ontological. A quick search revealed another fun example of this dual nature — <em>arkhe</em> is the root of both an "archetype," or original version of something, as well as "monarchy," "oligarchy," and "hierarchy" — different forms of power.</span>
<span class="trinity-comment-count">0</span>
</div>
