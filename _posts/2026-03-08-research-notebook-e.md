I was pleasantly surprised when I found out that our sample corpus for the workshop would be presidential inaugural speeches, as presidential history is likely the area of history I have the most knowledge of. I tried to think of creative ways to apply my knowledge, but ultimately struggled to come up with anything novel from my knowledge. 

As I pondered where to start, I thought back to Ramsay’s deeplying engaging and relatable reading about the joys of “screwing around” in the archives. I sympathized with Ramsay’s relishing in scouring archives without a strategy in mind, and with his goal to discover “how digital humanities might reform certain academic orthodoxies that work against the hermeneutics of screwing around.” The article also made me consider to what extent “screwing around” is a crutch for us as historians? As I was struggling to think of a historically interesting term search in the inaugural address, I had to acknowledge that it probably has been one for me. It is freeing to be able to roam through archives, grabbing at words that interest you until something meaningful arises from it. It is much harder to sit alone with your existing knowledge until you arrive at a strategy. I confronted difficulty of sparking ideas without some kind of “screwing around” to serve as inspiration when beginning this workshop. Despite the fact that I know more about the lives of certain presidents more than I know about most of my family members, when I tried to think of interesting words to search I came up blank. 

Eventually, I decided on a basic starting point; a kind of half-measure which I hoped would trigger more inspiration. In the same vain that Cameron Blevins used words associated with “cold weather” as a kind of litmus test for their programs accuracy in “Topic Modeling Martha Ballard’s Diary,” I would begin by using the word 
“war” in mine. The results were not what I expected:

With the exception of Lincoln’s presidency, there was no clear trend of the word spiking during times of war. However, because of my dataset this did not lead me to question the reliability of my data but to think about the reasons why this occurred. I discussed with the people at my table how this trend could indicate a desire of politicians to not discuss conflict in explicit terms, perhaps a result of the audience of the speeches themselves changing in the age of radio and television as opposed to in older eras when the primary audience was Congress itself.

Following this initial foray, I ultimately decided to go a different route, turning my focus on the potentially more significant word: “Americans.” My initial prediction was that there would be an increase in the word over time, but the results were more extreme than I could have predicted:

The term did not appear at all in the speeches until 1909, and then rose gradually, with hills and valleys along the way, to peak around the end of the cold war. My first thought when deciding on my term and seeing the results of my search had been how the data might chart the rise of American national identity. And while I think there is something to this thesis, the truth is much more nuanced. For example, the first instance of the word being used was by William Howard Taft in 1909 in the context of four Pan-American Conference, an event which Taft said “will have a special meaning to the hearts of all Americans.” Although this initial use would seem to carry connotations of Pan-American identity, the remaining 16 appearances of “Americans” were decidedly referring to United States citizens. Often, these citizens were abroad and contrasted with their foreign environment, as with two Americans who had recently been executed in Nicaragua, triggering military response. Here we see the connections between the rise of American identity and the desire to protect American citizens using the nation’s military might. The term next surfaced in 1917 when Woodrow Wilson, laying the foundations of the future of American foreign policy, declared that the ongoing war in Europe had “made us citizens of the world…And yet we are not the less Americans on that account. We shall be the more American if we but remain true to the principles in which we have been bred.” As with Taft, Wilson connects the spirit of nationalism with a sense of moral interventionism and American exceptionalism. 

In contrast to other text-mining efforts described by Jockers and Underwood, which have sought to maximize the “representativeness” of a corpus in order to make conclusions about a group of people over a period of time, the inaugural addresses are better described as “a few hand‐selected examples” (Underwood, 301). What can we learn about American culture at large by analyzing the words of Presidents. Even if these leaders represented the average American, which they surely do not, how can we connect their words to broader cultural change? Can we safely assume that their was a rise of American national identity precipitated by global foreign affairs, or is this only one dimension that would inevitably be overemphasized in a speech delivered by the head of state? 

Cameron Blevins’ article on Martha Ballard’s Diary demonstrates the importance of knowing your corpus, and the problems that arise for historians when “knowing your corpus” can only go so far. After all, one major purpose of text-mining programs it to analyze massive amounts of text that humans couldn’t possibly analyze. However, the human running a text-mining program still needs to be knowledgeable enough to know to, for example, search for terms like “the Author of all my Mercies” in addition to “God.” Applying this to my situation in examining the inaugural addresses, I considered the ways in which text-mining without needed context could lead to false conclusions. For one, the nature of the corpus itself should be considered: what are the inaugural addresses? What is their purpose? What is a “standard” inaugural address, and what can this information tell us about how to utilize them historically? If the purpose of an inaugural address is to preview policy and set goals, then perhaps it is only to be expected that national identity would be framed in terms of foreign policy, even if these concerns were not always at the forefront of Americans’ minds.

	These considerations demonstrated both the potential and potential harms of text-mining that this weeks authors had reviewed. As I continue to use text-mining in my own research with softwares such as HathiTrust, this exercise had given me pause to consider the implications of my historical work. It is all too tempting to start with a narrative and then use the number that pops up to justify it. But we as historians must remember to consider a myriad of dimensions in all such analysis. We must not utilize this technology at the expense of our own familiarity with the reality of our sources.
